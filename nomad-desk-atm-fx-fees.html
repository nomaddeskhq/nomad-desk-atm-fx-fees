Avoiding ATM and Currency Exchange Fees on Long-Term Travel

Nomad Desk Editorial · long-stay departures board

A single ATM withdrawal fee looks small on a receipt. Multiply it by weekly withdrawals across a year on a digital nomad visa, and it becomes one of the largest silent costs of long-term travel — usually bigger than the visa fee itself, and far less discussed.

Three separate fees are stacked on top of each other

Every foreign withdrawal or card swipe can carry up to three distinct charges: your home bank's foreign transaction fee, the local ATM operator's withdrawal fee, and a "dynamic currency conversion" markup if you accept being charged in your home currency instead of the local one. Declining dynamic conversion and always choosing to be charged in the local currency removes the worst of the three, but the other two still apply unless your card is specifically fee-free abroad.

Withdraw less often, in larger amounts, from bank-affiliated machines

Flat per-withdrawal fees reward fewer, larger withdrawals over frequent small ones. Machines physically attached to a bank branch tend to have lower or no surcharge compared with standalone ATMs in tourist areas, airports, or convenience stores, which often carry the highest surcharges precisely because they know travelers have no other option nearby.

Fee structures vary a lot by destination

How painful this is depends heavily on where you are and how long you are staying, which is exactly the kind of destination-specific detail Nomad Desk's briefings try to date and source rather than generalize. The Portugal briefing and Thailand briefing both note local banking norms alongside visa requirements, and Mexico's briefing flags where cash remains more common than card payment, which changes how often you will need to withdraw at all.

Card choice matters more than any single trip habit

The single highest-leverage decision is which card you carry, not how carefully you use it. A small number of travel-focused debit and credit cards waive foreign transaction fees and ATM surcharges entirely or refund them monthly; carrying at least one as a backup, alongside your primary card, avoids being stuck with a bad-fee card if your main one is lost, blocked, or unsupported at a given machine.

None of this works without connectivity to check rates

Comparing real exchange rates against what a machine or merchant is quoting you requires being online in the moment, which is why Nomad Desk keeps a dated eSIM guide as a companion to its money-related briefings — and a best-time-to-visit guide for Japan for travelers timing a trip around both weather and budget.

The bottom line

Fee avoidance on long-term travel is mostly about card selection and withdrawal habits, not clever tricks. Always decline dynamic currency conversion, withdraw larger amounts less often from bank-affiliated machines, and carry a backup fee-free card.
